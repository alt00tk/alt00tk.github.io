title: "Can We Really Learn from the Outcomes of Our Decisions?"
description: "Even rational choices can lead to bad outcomes. What can we learn from experience without equating a bad outcome with a mistaken decision?"

---

When I think about what makes a good decision, I feel the need to distinguish between its rationality at the time and how well it turns out.
What, then, can we learn when we experience a bad outcome?

## Does a Failure Mean the Design Decision Was Wrong?

Suppose, for example, that we compared two system designs: a low-cost configuration with an estimated failure probability of around 1%, and a more expensive configuration that reduces the risk.
We chose the former after considering the probability of failure, its impact, and the cost.
But a failure occurred soon after the system went into production.

[Baron and Hershey (1988)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.54.4.569) experimentally demonstrated “outcome bias”: the tendency to rate a decision more favorably when its outcome is good, even when the information available at the time of the decision is the same.

If we had accepted the risk based on the information available then, the failure alone does not mean we made the wrong choice.
We do not know whether the more expensive configuration would have prevented it, either.
Under the same conditions, I would choose the lower-cost design again.

Even so, understanding the conditions under which the failure occurred and its impact would teach us something new.
If we find a low-cost way to address the failure, we may be able to improve the system without changing its configuration.

## How Should We Treat an Outcome That Differs from Our Prediction?

How, then, should we treat that estimated 1% probability of failure?
I would not revise the 1% estimate just because a failure occurred when the system first went into production. Instead, I would reexamine the evidence behind it.

A 1% estimate based on 10,000 past observations under equivalent conditions warrants a different level of confidence from a rough 1% estimate based on experience with similar systems.
[Griffin and Tversky (1992)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(92)90013-R) reported that people tend to give too little consideration to the weight of evidence, such as sample size, relative to its apparent strength.

However, if operating conditions differ, even a large body of past observations cannot be used directly as a basis for the estimate.
We may give more weight to a smaller set of observations under matching conditions, but uncertainty in the estimate remains.

## Learning and Responding to Changing Circumstances

If a customer requested measures to prevent recurrence after the failure, I would decide to implement them.
That would not necessarily be because I had learned something new about the failure, but because a condition of the decision—the customer's requirements—had changed.

We may gain knowledge from an outcome without changing our decision, and our decision may change with the circumstances even without new knowledge.
For now, I want to think of learning as comparing outcomes with our prior predictions and assumptions to see what, if anything, we have learned.

Still, I do not yet have an answer to how much weight we should give a single outcome, or how to distinguish chance from an error in our estimate.
