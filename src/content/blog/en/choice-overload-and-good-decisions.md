A tasting display with 24 varieties of jam attracted more customers than one with six.
Yet among those who stopped, the purchase rate was lower at the display with 24 varieties.
This was an experiment by [Iyengar and Lepper (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995).

Perhaps more varieties mean more appealing candidates, making it difficult to settle on one.
Do people worry about regretting the options they pass up, or give up on comparing them altogether? Those were my first thoughts.

## Are Fewer Options Better?

Wouldn't a display of 24 varieties make people want to stop even if they had no intention of buying?
Perhaps the purchase rate was lower because it attracted more people who had never intended to buy, rather than because they could not choose.
This is only my hypothesis, not an explanation established by the experiment.

[Scheibehenne et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1086/651235) combined 50 experiments and reported that the average negative effect of choice overload was close to zero, with considerable variation across studies.
It would be premature to generalize from the jam experiment alone that fewer options are better.

## What Makes Choosing Difficult?

[Chernev et al. (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002) identify four factors: the complexity of the options, the difficulty of the decision task, uncertainty about preferences, and the goal of the decision.
The number of options matters, but so do what we are choosing for and what we care about.

Would it help, then, to decide on evaluation criteria first?
I find it more natural to propose provisional criteria as hypotheses and update them while comparing candidates, rather than fixing the criteria at the outset.
[Bettman et al. (1998)](https://doi.org/10.1086/209535) also argue that people's preferences are constructed during the process of choosing.

That does not mean we can change the criteria to suit whichever candidate we favor.
When choosing a technology, for example, there is value we want to deliver and requirements that must be met to deliver it.
If we revise the criteria during a comparison, we need to return to those higher-level values and requirements.

Even having criteria does not guarantee a fair comparison.
[Russo et al. (1998)](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224379803500403) report that once a candidate takes the lead during a decision, evaluations of subsequent information become distorted in its favor.

## What Counts as a Good Decision?

To begin with, does a higher purchase rate indicate better decisions?
Choosing not to buy can also be rational.
[Baron and Hershey (1988)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.54.4.569) report “outcome bias,” in which the quality of an outcome influences how the decision itself is evaluated.

Good outcomes are not the only thing I want to value.
I also want to be able to explain the reasons for a decision given the knowledge available at the time and constraints such as time and cost, and afterward to trace the evidence behind it and identify the alternatives I passed over.
Even when the outcome is poor, there is value in retaining lessons that can inform the next decision.

Of course, being able to explain a decision does not mean it was sound.
I began by wondering why having many options might make it difficult to choose. As I followed the research, I came to see that outcomes also depend on the purpose and criteria of a decision, and on how information is interpreted.
Before debating the number of options, I want to consider what counts as a “good decision” in the first place.
