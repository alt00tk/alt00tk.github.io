Suppose we are comparing two designs for a business application. Design A would save customers a total of 100 hours of work per month, while design B would save the development team 9 hours of maintenance work per month.
Which should we choose?

Although both are measured in “hours,” we cannot say that the 100 hours are more valuable.
I want to start by considering what basis we have for comparing the two.

## From Total Hours to Business Impact

At first, I thought we could translate both benefits into value for the organization as a whole.
But 100 hours spread across many users means something different from 9 hours of maintenance that strains a small development team’s capacity.

I would therefore want to investigate whether the current bottleneck lies in customers’ operations or our own development, and which constraints would become tighter as usage grows.
I would also distinguish forgone benefits from maintenance costs that actually arise and risks that may emerge in the future.

Value-Focused Thinking, as described by [Keeney (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0377-2217(96)00004-5), is an approach that clarifies what we ultimately want to achieve before considering alternatives.
I think what I wanted to compare was less the number of hours itself than each option’s effect on the business’s ability to continue and grow.

## Rethinking the Role of Quantification

Still, examining how each option relates to broader objectives does not necessarily tell us which is better.
If both customer convenience and the development team’s capacity matter, we still have to decide which to prioritize.

Initially, I thought trying to combine different kinds of value into a single formula was problematic.
But might it be useful to vary provisional weights and see at what point our preferred option changes from A to B or vice versa?
The sensitivity analysis discussed by [Triantaphyllou and Sánchez (1997)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-5915.1997.tb01306.x) also examines how changes in weights and evaluation scores affect the choice.

We can use quantification less to produce the right answer than to check which assumptions our judgment depends on and where it breaks down.
Seen this way, quantification takes on a different meaning.

## The Value Judgment That Remains

Are the provisional weights reasonable in light of actual costs and effort? Do they fit the current business direction? Can we bear the loss if our judgment turns out to be wrong?
After checking these points, I suppose we would have to decide which value to prioritize as an organization.

For now, when comparing different values, I want to clarify their impact on our objectives and the conditions under which our choice would switch, without relying solely on conversion to a common scale.
But being satisfied with our decision does not, by itself, prove that it was rational.
I still do not know how thoroughly we should verify the assumptions that support our judgment.
