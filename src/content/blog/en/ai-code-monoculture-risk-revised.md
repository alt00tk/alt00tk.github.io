title: "Does AI-Generated Code Reproduce Shared Vulnerabilities?"
description: "If AI makes the internal structure of code more alike, could it also spread unsafe implementations? I consider the relationship between homogenization and verification."

The more we delegate implementation to AI, could code become more similar not only in how it is written, but also in how responsibilities and dependencies are organized?
The same AI model seems likely to choose similar solutions.

## Does Similarity Improve Quality?

At first, I was considering whether homogenization improves quality.
Similar structures are neither good nor bad in themselves.
Fewer defects would be better, and if a weakness is shared, perhaps it becomes easier to identify the danger and spread countermeasures.

But that is after the weakness has been found.
If unknown defects are shared too, they could continue to go unnoticed.
Nor does AI necessarily choose similar structures because they are of higher quality.

## Could Unsafe Implementations Be Reproduced Too?

That led me to another concern.
AI may have learned unsafe code patterns and may generate them.
If the code it generates is also similar, could the same kinds of weaknesses find their way into different products?

These are two separate hypotheses.
The problem of shared vulnerabilities arises when the generation of dangerous patterns coincides with their widespread reproduction.

Copying and shared libraries also spread weaknesses.
With a shared library, it may be possible to identify a common source to fix, but similar code generated separately has no common source of updates.
The same problem occurs with copying, but as the scale of AI generation grows, could tracking which products still contain the same weakness itself become difficult?

## Can Verification Keep Up with Generation?

Another issue is human involvement.
While AI generates code rapidly, people may have fewer opportunities to understand its internal structure.
My concern is less about a decline in people's abilities themselves than the possibility of problems being missed as the volume of code to review grows and human involvement thins out.

[Lee et al. (2025)](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.10/) report a tendency toward low diversity in the solutions used by generated code, while [Pearce et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1109/SP46214.2022.9833571) report instances of unsafe code generated by Copilot at the time.
In an experiment by [Perry et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3576915.3623157), participants who received AI assistance also tended to consider their code secure despite writing less secure code.

These studies do not, however, establish the entire causal chain I am considering here.
I do not know whether today's AI is increasing shared vulnerabilities or whether verification is missing more problems.

Having humans understand every piece of code is not an end in itself.
If verification works sufficiently well, the concern becomes smaller.
For now, what concerns me is whether verification can keep pace with the speed at which dangerous implementations could be generated widely.
I also have yet to answer what should count as sufficient verification.
