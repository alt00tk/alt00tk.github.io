I have been delegating implementation to AI more often.
Even without writing the code myself, I can achieve results faster than before.
But can I take those results as evidence that my own abilities have improved?

## Isn't Making Use of the Environment an Ability Too?

At first, I thought that achieving results by using AI and other tools was itself part of a person's ability.
There is also the ability to build an environment in which results can be achieved.

Still, if AI simply adapts to a new technology on its own, I feel that this does not mean the person is highly adaptable.
On the other hand, what if they compare and test several approaches themselves, then switch environments?
Even without understanding every internal detail of the technology, their own adaptability should be evident there.

## Choosing and Exercising Judgment Are Different

Then can adopting an AI suggestion also be considered an exercise of one's own ability?

By "judgment" here, I mean independently evaluating the validity of options in light of goals, constraints, and evidence, then deciding whether to accept them.
Simply choosing an option presented by AI makes it difficult to say that someone has exercised judgment.
Conversely, even if AI proposes the evaluation criteria themselves, it seems fair to say that a person has exercised their own judgment if they examine and adopt those criteria.

However, exercising judgment and exercising sound judgment are different things.
A wrong conclusion can still involve judgment, and a good outcome does not necessarily mean the decision-making process was sound.

## Can Judgment Be Evaluated from the Outside?

[Baron and Hershey (1988)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.54.4.569) experimentally demonstrated "outcome bias," in which the quality of an outcome affects the evaluation of a decision. I want to place weight not just on results, but on whether a judgment was reasonable given the information available at the time.

Would keeping conversation logs be enough, then?

[Nisbett and Wilson (1977)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.84.3.231) argue that people cannot necessarily explain their own cognitive processes accurately. Even a record of interactions with AI cannot fully capture what someone understood or how they thought.

It is difficult to observe the process of judgment accurately.
Even so, I want to consider which abilities engineers will need, and be evaluated on, in an age when AI handles implementation.
A clearer picture should offer clues about what I need to develop myself and also help me support less experienced engineers.

For now, I consider it important not only to be able to design and implement independently, but also to make use of an environment that includes AI, judge the validity of proposals, and manage quality and risk.
That includes putting verification methods in place for parts I cannot fully understand, recognizing the limits, and turning to experts when necessary.
But what evidence can be used to evaluate these abilities, and how accurately, remains unresolved.
